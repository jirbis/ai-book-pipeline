# Style Guide — The Science of Decision-Making

## Voice and Tone

- **Overall tone**: Conversational, curious, authoritative
- **Voice description**: Warm, precise, engaging, accessible, grounded
- **Voice samples**:
  - ✅ Correct: "Loss aversion is the tendency to feel losses more sharply than equivalent gains — roughly twice as sharply, according to Kahneman and Tversky's estimates."
  - ❌ Incorrect: "Loss aversion, a well-documented phenomenon in the behavioral economics literature, refers to the asymmetric valuation of losses relative to commensurate gains."

## Addressing the Reader

- **Pronouns**: Use "you" for the reader, "we" when sharing a universal human experience, "I" sparingly and only in personal anecdotes
- **Inclusive language**: Use gender-neutral examples; vary the demographics of hypothetical characters; avoid assumptions about the reader's background

## Vocabulary

### Technical Terms

| Term                  | Definition (on first use)                                             |
|-----------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Heuristic             | A mental shortcut that simplifies decision-making                      |
| Cognitive bias        | A systematic pattern of deviation from rational judgment               |
| Anchoring             | The tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information     |
| Prospect theory       | A model describing how people choose between probabilistic outcomes    |
| Loss aversion         | The tendency to prefer avoiding losses over acquiring equivalent gains |
| Framing effect        | When the way information is presented alters a decision                |
| Decision fatigue      | The deteriorating quality of decisions after a long session            |
| Choice architecture   | The design of environments in which people make choices                |
| Temporal discounting  | Valuing immediate rewards more than future ones                        |

### Marker Words to Avoid

| Avoid             | Prefer                            |
|-------------------|-----------------------------------|
| utilize           | use                               |
| paradigm          | model, framework                  |
| leverage (verb)   | use, take advantage of            |
| impact (verb)     | affect, influence                 |
| optimal           | best, most effective              |
| robust (informal) | strong, reliable                  |
| basically         | (delete — adds nothing)           |
| obviously         | (delete — sounds condescending)   |

### Preferred Words

| Prefer            | Instead of                        |
|-------------------|-----------------------------------|
| choice            | selection (when casual)           |
| mistake           | error (in casual context)         |
| find / discover   | ascertain                         |
| show              | demonstrate (in casual context)   |

## Grammar and Punctuation

- **Oxford comma**: Yes, always
- **Quotation marks**: Double quotes for direct quotes; single quotes for terms used in a special sense on first mention (e.g., what psychologists call 'satisficing')
- **Dashes**: Em-dash (—) without spaces for interjections; en-dash (–) for ranges (pp. 12–15, 2010–2020)
- **Numbers**: Spell out one through nine; use numerals for 10 and above; always use numerals with units (3 percent, 7 seconds) and in data contexts
- **Percentages**: Use "percent" in running text, "%" in tables and figures
- **Verb tense**: Present tense for describing research findings ("Kahneman shows…"); past tense for historical events ("In 1974, Tversky and Kahneman conducted…")

## Formatting

### Headings
- `#` — Chapter title only
- `##` — Major sections within a chapter
- `###` — Subsections
- `####` — Rare; only for labeled boxes (e.g., "#### Decision Toolkit")

### Emphasis
- **Bold** for key terms on first formal introduction
- *Italic* for book titles, emphasis within a sentence, and foreign words
- `Code` — not used (this is not a technical book)

### Lists
- Bulleted lists for unordered items (tips, examples)
- Numbered lists only when sequence matters (steps in a process)
- Keep list items parallel in grammatical structure

### Blockquotes
- Use `>` for direct quotes from sources (with attribution)
- Do not use blockquotes for the author's own emphasis

## Genre Specifics (Non-Fiction)

### Source Citations
- **Style**: In-text narrative citations — "(Kahneman, 2011)" or "As Kahneman (2011) argues…"
- **Full references**: Collected in bibliography.md; APA 7th edition format
- **Footnotes**: Avoid; integrate citations into running text or use endnotes sparingly for tangential details

### Examples Format
- **Pattern**: Story → Science → Takeaway
  1. Open with a concrete story or scenario
  2. Explain the underlying research or principle
  3. Close with a practical implication or action step

### Chapter Structure
1. Opening hook (story / scenario / provocative question)
2. Context and background
3. 2–3 core concepts with examples
4. Practical application ("Decision Toolkit" box)
5. Bridge to the next chapter

## Taboos

### Never Use
- Jargon without immediate plain-language definition
- Passive voice when active is possible
- Rhetorical questions more than twice per chapter section
- Block quotes longer than 4 lines (paraphrase instead)

### Use with Caution
- Personal anecdotes (max 1–2 per chapter, brief)
- Humor (keep it light, never at a group's expense)
- Pop-culture references (they date quickly — prefer timeless examples)

## Style Checklist

### For Writing
- [ ] Does the opening hook grab attention within the first paragraph?
- [ ] Is every technical term defined on first use?
- [ ] Does each section alternate between story and explanation?
- [ ] Is the "Decision Toolkit" box present before the chapter's final paragraph?
- [ ] Are all claims backed by a cited source?

### For Editing
- [ ] Is the voice consistent with author-voice.md?
- [ ] Are sentences varied in length (short + long)?
- [ ] Are transitions smooth between sections?
- [ ] Are all numbers, dates, and names consistent?
- [ ] Has passive voice been minimized?

## Examples and Samples

### Sample Paragraph (Non-Fiction — Technical Explanation)

> When you walk into a car dealership, the first number you hear — the sticker price — quietly rewires your expectations. Even if you know the markup is absurd, that number becomes the starting point for every calculation that follows. Psychologists call this **anchoring**, and it's one of the most reliable findings in all of behavioral science. In study after study, arbitrary numbers — a roulette wheel spin, a Social Security digit, the asking price on a house — drag people's estimates toward them, whether those people are students, professionals, or experts (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).

## Notes

# Author Voice — The Science of Decision-Making

## General Characteristics

- **Tone**: Warm, curious, conversational — like a knowledgeable friend explaining something fascinating over coffee
- **One-sentence description**: Clear, engaging science writing that treats readers as intelligent adults while never assuming specialized knowledge

## Lexical Features

### Preferred Words and Expressions
- "Consider this…" / "Here's the thing…" / "It turns out that…"
- Active, concrete verbs: "reveals," "drives," "shapes," "triggers"
- Everyday analogies before technical terms
- First-person plural ("we") to include the reader in the exploration

### Avoided Words
- Overblown superlatives ("revolutionary," "groundbreaking") unless quoting
- Academic hedge piles ("it might possibly be the case that…")
- Buzzwords without substance ("synergy," "paradigm shift")

### Formality Level
- Middle register: more polished than a blog post, less formal than a journal article
- Contractions are fine ("don't," "it's," "we're")
- Technical terms always defined on first use, in plain language

## Syntactic Features

### Sentence Length
- Average 15-20 words; mix of short punchy sentences (5-8 words) with longer explanatory ones (25-30 words)
- Short sentences for emphasis after a longer passage: "That changes everything."

### Sentence Structure
- Favor subject-verb-object; avoid long subordinate clause chains
- Use rhetorical questions to transition between ideas: "So why do we keep falling for it?"
- Occasional fragment for rhythm: "Not even close."

## Rhythm and Pace

- **Narrative pace**: Moderate — unhurried explanations, but never lingering too long on one point
- **Rhythm techniques**: Alternate between story (slow) and science (faster, denser); end sections with a short, declarative sentence
- **Pauses and accents**: Em-dashes for interjections; short paragraphs (2-4 sentences) to maintain visual pace

## Tone and Mood

- **Predominant mood**: Intellectual curiosity mixed with gentle humor
- **Tone variations**:
  - Opening stories: narrative, immersive
  - Research explanations: precise, enthusiastic
  - Practical takeaways: direct, encouraging
  - Occasional light irony when describing human foibles

## Stylistic Devices

- **Metaphors**: Drawn from everyday life — kitchens, roads, weather, sports — never from obscure literary or classical references
- **Repetition**: Deliberate callback to a chapter's opening story in its closing paragraph
- **Figures of speech**: Light irony, understatement; avoid heavy sarcasm
- **Analogies**: Central device — every abstract concept gets at least one concrete analogy

## Intentional Deviations

- Starting a sentence with "And" or "But" for conversational flow
- Occasional sentence fragments for emphasis
- Parenthetical asides for humor or quick clarifications

## Taboos

### Forbidden
- Condescending tone ("As everyone knows…", "Obviously…")
- Passive constructions when an active one is available
- Footnote-heavy academic style
- Unattributed claims presented as fact

### Unacceptable Devices
- Excessive exclamation marks
- Clickbait-style rhetorical tricks ("You won't believe what happened next")
- Overly long block quotes (paraphrase and cite instead)

## Reference Text Samples

### Sample 1 — Opening a Chapter

> You're standing in the cereal aisle. There are forty-seven options. Your brain, which evolved to track predators on the savanna, is now trying to evaluate fiber content, price per ounce, and whether your kids will actually eat the stuff. You grab the same box you bought last week and move on. The whole episode took nine seconds.
>
> That nine-second cereal grab is a masterpiece of mental engineering — a heuristic, in the language of cognitive science. Your brain skipped the spreadsheet and used a rule of thumb: *go with what worked before*. Most of the time, that rule works beautifully. Sometimes, though, it leads you straight into a trap.

**Key features**: Concrete scenario, second-person immersion, short punchy sentence, italicized rule, foreshadowing of the chapter's conflict.

### Sample 2 — Explaining Research

> In 1974, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman ran one of the most cited experiments in psychology. They spun a rigged roulette wheel in front of their subjects — it always landed on either 10 or 65 — and then asked an unrelated question: "What percentage of African countries are in the United Nations?" People who saw the wheel land on 65 guessed much higher than those who saw 10. The random number had *anchored* their thinking, pulling their estimates toward it like gravity.
>
> This is the anchoring effect, and it's disturbingly robust. It works on real-estate agents pricing homes, judges setting bail, and shoppers evaluating discounts. Knowing about it doesn't make you immune. But it does give you a fighting chance.

**Key features**: Historical narrative, specific details, italicized key term, everyday examples, closing with practical implication.

### Sample 3 — Practical Takeaway

> Here's a decision you can make right now, before you finish this chapter: start a decision journal. It doesn't need to be fancy — a notes app works fine. Before any significant choice, write down three things: what you're deciding, what you expect to happen, and why. Then revisit it in a month. You'll start noticing patterns — the situations where your instincts are sharp, and the ones where they consistently mislead you.

**Key features**: Direct address, actionable advice, low barrier to entry, conversational parenthetical, forward-looking motivation.

## Instructions for Agents

### WRITER
- Open every chapter with a scene or story, not a thesis statement
- Alternate between narrative passages and explanatory passages
- Use "we" and "you" liberally; avoid "one" as a pronoun
- Insert a "Decision Toolkit" box before each chapter's final paragraph

### EDITOR
- Do NOT flatten sentence variety — the mix of short and long is intentional
- Do NOT remove conversational fragments ("Not quite.", "Here's why.")
- Preserve em-dash interjections and parenthetical asides
- Ensure every technical term is defined on first use

### PROOFREADER
- Flag, do not auto-correct: intentional fragments, sentences starting with "And"/"But"
- Verify that anchoring stories and research citations match sources
- Ensure consistent use of en-dash (–) for ranges, em-dash (—) for interjections
